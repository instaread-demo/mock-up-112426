Real estate’s titans are spending millions on art, watches and joyrides 

Money made in brick and mortar, glass and steel buys superyachts and Rolls-Royces for industry’s big names

It’s a familiar scene for Stephanie Shojaee’s 631,000 Instagram followers: Stephanie and her husband, Masoud Shojaee, stepping out of their his-and-hers Rolls-Royces on the tarmac. They climb the stairs of their private jet, painted a custom shade of gray to match the $400,000 cars and bearing the logo of their Miami-based real estate development firm, Shoma Group. 

“When is enough enough? I don’t know. I want a his and hers airplane. I want superyachts,” Stephanie Shojaee said in a recent podcast interview. “Of course I love fabulous things. I love diamonds and watches and all that stuff.” 

Real estate is a ruthless game. One poorly timed deal or market downturn can topple an empire; it’s a tale familiar to longtime readers of The Real Deal. But to the victors go the spoils: The Forbes and Bloomberg billionaire rankings feature hundreds of real estate moguls from around the world. 

The industry’s titans are spending their millions on shiny treasures: yachts, planes, cars, art, bags, watches and memorabilia. They’re not alone — as the ultrawealthy population grows larger and richer, spending in luxury toy industries is up too. 

The Knight Frank Luxury Investment Index, which tracks prices across art, watches, designer bags and collectible wine and whisky, rose more than 38 percent over the past decade. Auctions for these products are seeing unprecedented sales. Car collectors spent a record $758 million at the Monterey Car Week auctions in August, and New York’s November Gigaweek just saw a near-record $2 billion in sales.

Besides her cars and plane, Stephanie Shojaee likes to share her Birkin collection. Hermès famously restricts the purchasing process for its most famous handbag, and depending on the make and model, prices can range from $13,500 to millions.

Todd Glaser, the Palm Beach-based spec developer, wasn’t at this year’s historic Monterey Car Week. But last year he scooped up a $1.4 million 1962 Ferrari 250 GT Cabriolet Series II by Pininfarina, a $1.3 million 1965 Ferrari 275 GTS by Pininfarina and a $302,000 1971 Mercedes 280 SE 3.5 V8. He also cruises the Intercoastal Waterway on the Sea Tabby, his 1938 Mathis Trumpy 61-foot yacht. 

Bob Knakal collects Louis Vuitton sneakers (he’s got about 30 pairs in his closet, he confirmed), but the broker and Rangers season ticket holder of 40 years is most passionate about his trove of Mark Messier game-worn hockey jerseys. At one point, his collection included 62 jerseys, though he’s whittled it down to a dirty dozen — the authenticity of the jerseys is verified by the scuffs, tears and puck marks they earn during play. Prices vary, but he’s spent as much as $150,000 for a jersey.

Why Messier? “He led the Rangers to their first cup in 1994,” Knakal said. “Fifty-four years, no cups, and he led them to the cup.”

Lost toys

Flashy toys can also attract the sticky-fingered. 

Jason Oppenheim, who heads the Oppenheim Group in California, doubles as a car collector. The brokerage owner and “Selling Sunset” star’s holdings over the years have included a Tesla Model Y with a starting price of $40,000, a restored vintage Ford Bronco, a limited-run FF 91 2.0 Futurist Alliance from Faraday Future that launched for $309,000 in 2023 and a Ferrari Roma valued at about $275,000. 

But in March, he reported his $500,000 Rolls-Royce SUV stolen from the parking lot of his West Hollywood office, according to published reports. 

Famously, in 2022, Cardone Capital boss Grant Cardone filed a lawsuit against Steve Ross’ Hard Rock Stadium after his $750,000 Richard Mille watch was stolen while he attended the Formula One Miami Grand Prix.

Cardone dropped the suit in 2023, but he’s got plenty of watches to spare. He regularly shows off a million-dollar assemblage of Patek Philippe, Panerai and Richard Mille watches on social media, in addition to his Bombardier Global 7500 and Gulfstream G650ER jets.

“I would never give that plane up. I’m giving my kids up,” he said in an interview with the Iced Coffee Hour podcast about the Gulfstream. 

After Donald Trump threatened to ban the sale of Bombardiers in the U.S. in September, Cardone announced on X that he’d sell that plane for $80 million. “Supply & Demand — Thank you sir,” he wrote.

There are thieves, and then there are the Feds and creditors. Just as spectacular as the rise can be in this industry, so too is the fall. 

Last year, when Fortress Investment Group sought to claw back a $187 million personally guaranteed loan from Charles Cohen, the firm launched legal efforts to seize his 25 luxury cars, five yachts and chateau in Provence, as well as his art and wine collections. He paid off the debt in June, but is now seeking $204 million in damages in a suit that accuses Fortress of harassing his family. 

Rishi Kapoor, the Miami developer and former Location Ventures CEO, saw his 68-foot yacht seized by the U.S. Marshals in 2023 as part of a fraud investigation. The Viking Princess he named “M/Y Suneeta II” has since been turned over to his lender, and in September a federal judge sentenced him to 11 years in prison.

The long game

Other real estate titans are more legacy-minded when it comes to spending on their toys. 

For a long time, Related Group chairman Jorge Pérez and his wife Darlene kept his favorite painting, the Joan Mitchell triptych “Iva,” a 1973 oil on canvas, hanging in their bedroom. Now, it’s on display at the Tate Museum in London.

The $3.3 million artwork was one of more than 5,000 pieces the couple collected in their lifetime before they gifted it to the Tate last year. The assemblage made Jorge Pérez one of the world’s top art collectors, a hobby funded by the multi-billion-dollar fortune he built developing Miami’s condo towers.

He’s given hundreds of pieces to the Pérez Art Museum Miami, Miami’s premier art museum that was renamed after him in 2011. His combined donations of art and cash total $80 million, according to published reports. 

Now he’s expanding his art territory to Aspen. Records show Pérez paid $24.5 million for a 200-acre property at 1500 Ridge of Wildcat Drive in Snowmass in 2024. This year, he unveiled his private sculpture garden at the estate, with works by Fernando Botero, Paloma Varga Weisz and Jim Dine set against a dramatic view of the Rockies in a spread for Cultured Magazine. 

He told the outlet, “I still have some negotiating to do with the family, but I’d love for the whole collection and the house to become a kind of artist colony and residency.”

Until that deal is settled, he can take in the 2002 Deborah Butterfield sculpture “Lark,” a bronze recreation of a horse bending its head to the ground, through his living room window.
