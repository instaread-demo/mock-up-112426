Anti-Abortion States Ask Supreme Court To Nullify Shield Laws in Blue States

By

Rewire News Group Staff

Published:

Oct 2, 2026
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 2:24
Instaread

The laws protect providers who mail abortion medication to patients in other states. Plus, Arkansas’ abortion ban is in court, and medical society urges OB-GYNs to protect their high-risk pregnant patients despite state restrictions.

Each week, Rewire News Group editors scour headlines nationwide—from lawsuits over abortion access to LGBTQ+ rights—to bring you the most urgent news in reproductive justice. Here’s what you need to know from this week’s biggest stories.

Louisiana, Alabama, and Arkansas sue to block telehealth abortions prescribed from shield law states

The Supreme Court may decide the fate of shield laws, which protect providers who prescribe abortion drugs to patients in ban states. Louisiana, Alabama, and Arkansas petitioned the Court on Wednesday, arguing that the laws—which many blue states enacted after the justices overturned Roe—are unconstitutional. If the Court agrees, people in ban states would largely lose access to mailed abortion pills. Two states sued in the complaint, New York and California, have used their shield laws to rebuff extradition requests.

Arkansas’ abortion ban violates right to life, liberty, and happiness: lawyers

On Monday, lawyers challenging Arkansas’ near-total abortion ban asked a judge to quash the state’s motion to dismiss. Six women denied abortions sued in early 2026, saying the law violated their right to life, liberty, and happiness. One began to bleed at 17 weeks pregnant; doctors found her amniotic sac and a foot coming out of her cervix. They said she’d lose the pregnancy but that they couldn’t induce labor until she got sicker. “I did it for my daughter,” she said of the lawsuit, “the one that’s still living and the one who passed.”

New medical advice aims to improve care, save lives of pregnant patients in states that limit abortion

Pregnant patients in states with abortion restrictions must still get “the very best care without unnecessary delay,” said a doctor from the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine earlier this month of the group’s new medical guidance. The guidelines advise high-risk OB-GYNs in ban states to build collaborative partnerships in states that allow abortion, expand access to abortion up to the legal limit allowed in their own state, and work to make birth control more accessible—including emergency contraception.
