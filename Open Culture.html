25 Films Inspired by Edgar Allan Poe Online: The Fall of the House of Usher, The Tell-Tale Heart, and More

Rank the films inspired by Edgar Allan Poe, and sev­er­al adap­ta­tions of “The Fall of the House of Ush­er” will almost cer­tain­ly come near the top. At first glance, that sto­ry of a man pay­ing a vis­it to an old friend who lives with his twin sis­ter in their fam­i­ly’s grand house — each of the lat­ter three well advanced into a dif­fer­ent process of dete­ri­o­ra­tion — might seem too short, as well as too light on con­flict to trans­late into a con­ven­tion­al screen­play. Yet it’s also full of cin­e­mat­i­cal­ly irre­sistible images, from the some­how ruinous yet whol­ly intact tit­u­lar man­sion to the blood­i­ly wretched sis­ter throw­ing her­self upon the ter­ri­fied broth­er who entombed her alive while their ances­tral home col­laps­es around them.

Of the twen­ty or thir­ty ver­sions of “The Fall of the House of Ush­er” so far made for the screen, the most cel­e­brat­ed remains the one Jean Epstein direct­ed in 1928. Offi­cial­ly des­ig­nat­ing it as one of the “great movies,” Roger Ebert calls that pic­ture’s vision of the great hall “one of the most haunt­ing spaces in the movies. Its floor is a vast mar­ble expanse, inter­rupt­ed here and there by an item of fur­ni­ture that seems dwarfed by the sur­round­ing empti­ness. An odd stair­case ris­es from one dis­tant cor­ner. It is not impos­si­ble that this vision, in one of the best-known French sur­re­al­ist films, inspired the design­ers of the great hall of Xanadu in Cit­i­zen Kane.”

The avant-garde film­mak­ing duo James Sib­ley Wat­son Jr. and Melville Web­ber (in col­lab­o­ra­tion with e. e. cum­mings, who con­tributed to the script) could hard­ly man­age the same scale of pro­duc­tion design for their own short adap­ta­tion of Poe’s sto­ry, which hap­pened to come out the same year.

But the result­ing Expres­sion­ist take on the mate­r­i­al has also been high­ly acclaimed, and through this YouTube playlist of Poe films, you can expe­ri­ence not just both of them, but also oth­er adap­ta­tions of “The Fall of the House of Ush­er” by idio­syn­crat­ic Czech ani­ma­tor Jan Švankma­jer and B‑movie pow­er­house Roger Cor­man. Sen­si­tive to Poe’s sen­sa­tion­al­ist appeal and air of trans­gres­sive deca­dence, Cor­man end­ed up direct­ing an eight-film “Poe cycle,” near­ly all of which star Vin­cent Price.

It’s fair to say that no film actor is more close­ly asso­ci­at­ed with Poe than Price, a link Tim Bur­ton cel­e­brat­ed in ani­ma­tion at the begin­ning of his career. Price even played the writer him­self in An Evening with Edgar Allan Poe, a spe­cial from 1970 in which he recites  “The Sphinx,” “The Cask of Amon­til­la­do,” “The Pit and the Pen­du­lum,” and “The Tell-Tale Heart.” That last, the most screen-adapt­ed Poe sto­ry of them all, also appears on the playlist in var­i­ous forms: an ear­ly D. W. Grif­fith fea­ture, the direc­to­r­i­al debut of Jules Dassin, an ani­mat­ed short nar­rat­ed by James Mason, a British psy­cho­log­i­cal thriller, and a trans­po­si­tion into mod­ern-day New Orleans. And if you haven’t had enough Poe after watch­ing through all 25 of these films, con­sid­er watch­ing the recent, well-received Fall of the House of Ush­er minis­eries on Net­flix — an enter­tain­ment plat­form that sat­is­fies much the same appetites Poe’s sto­ries have been feed­ing for near­ly two cen­turies.
