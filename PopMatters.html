Sympathy for the Devils in ‘You Can See Everything’ and ‘The Altruists’

Elizabeth Holmes’ film You Can See Everything and the Sam Bankman-Fried series The Altruists might be the finest propaganda since Leni Riefenstahl gilded the lily for the Nazis.

By
Sal Cataldi / 23 November 2026

When you’re rich and in the clink on a long sentence, tearful displays of regret on a network talk or news show, combined with behind-the-scenes machinations by your monied, powerful friends, can be the magic formula for engineering an early release. Now it seems the tech-billionaire class has an additional method that doesn’t require any such confession. It’s the big-budget movie or prestige television series – like You Can See Everything and The Altruists – that rewrites history for the public and pardon-lever pullers who are too busy to investigate the true ins and outs of the crimes.

The new film by the singularly talented Nathan Fielder about Theranos fraudster Elizabeth Holmes and Netflix’s ten-part series about FTX’s Sam Bankman-Fried and Caroline Ellison may just be the finest examples of propaganda since Leni Riefenstahl gilded the lily for the Third Reich. There’s no doubt that Fielder may be the John Coltrane of contemporary filmic content, a genius of revelatory long-form documentary (used lightly here) and faux comic fiction.

I don’t know if it is a pun, but calling his latest on Holmes, You Can See Everything, is misleading. Why? Because it seems to deliberately omit a central point: a brief but firm explanation, from a scientific authority, of why Holmes’ tech didn’t work and why she was found guilty in a long, costly trial.

Netflix’s The Altruists is a longer but equally entertaining revisionist retelling of the saga of disgraced crypto king Sam Bankman-Fried and his partner in crime and, at times, romance, Caroline Ellison (Julia Garner). This mini-series seems like a bold attempt to place most of the blame on Ellison and to paint Sam Bankman-Fried (Anthony Boyle) as the naive, virgin victim of his partner’s recklessness. It wants us to believe that Bankman-Fried was a man who walked the waters of FinTech, a Nerd Jesus, doing it all for charity, driven by his devotion to ethical altruism. If I could just get $100 for every time they banged the “EA” (Effective Altruism) drum in this ten-hour juggernaut, I would be a billionaire.

Both of these are slick modern storytelling designed to generate sympathy for the devil entrepreneurs and, maybe more importantly, cast “reasonable doubt” of the legal kind. Fielder’s You Can See Everything works to shift the blame and elicit sympathy for Holmes by adding a new character—the odious Billy Evans (Christopher Abbott)—into the mix. 

Holmes’ trust-fund baby daddy is a creep of the most controlling kind, especially after Elizabeth (Amanda Seyfried) goes off stage to her country club prison in Texas. Her absence lets Evans run wild, posting obscene comments and increasingly crazy defenses on her Twitter account and using his power of attorney to launch the Theranos encore that nobody but this couple wanted.

Evans is the classic professional wrestling heel; the bad guy you can’t take your eyes off as he digs the hole ever deeper. He is primarily designed, however, to do something more important. His antics and emotional outbursts divert your logical, judgmental mind from Holmes and her crimes. 

Evans also proselytizes for and glorifies her wonderful “weirdness”. He suggests that maybe, even if she were technically guilty, she was such a special brand of genius, a hyper-focused, otherworldly savant who couldn’t comprehend her guilt or truly be held accountable for it. The touching scenes with her young children in the days before she heads to jail, along with her nonstop waterworks, also soften her character. As for the tears, Fielder seemed moved a bit too much by them from my vantage.

On the other hand, Amanda Seyfried, who played Holmes in Elizabeth Meriwether’s 2022 bio series, The Dropout, and in You Can See Everything‘s reenactments, seems to buy none of it, though she sympathizes with Holmes over the control Evans exerts. Evans also offered Holmes’ wonderful weirdness as the explanation for her many awkward and evasive responses to the film’s big questions and her legendary bug-eyed stares, the biggest since the cheeseball paintings of Margaret Keane and Walter Keane.

Isn’t this a bad thing for Evans, to be this reviled character? From where I see it, it’s not. 

First off, even with his family wealth, Evans was a nobody before he hitched his cart to Holmes, just as her guilt began to surface. There’s a deep love of, and market for, heels in America: the unrepentant, deluded jerk who is way more entertaining and “clickable” than the self-effacing good guy or girl. For proof, we need look no further than the Oval Office and the weekly edition of WWE’s Monday Night Raw.

With Bankman-Fried, the case for sympathy may be slightly more deserved, given the whopping penalty. When the shit hit the fan with FTX, Ellison turned against Bankman-Fried, pinning the crimes she may have largely devised on him. She testified against him in court and got a comparatively light slap on the wrist, serving 14 months while Sam got 25 years.

She’s even back in the business, working for a tech charity platform named Manifold. She’ll likely never see a penny for her labor, as she is bankrupt and owes $11 billion in restitution for her crimes.

Then there’s the money issue dogging both projects. It’s still a bit fuzzy what ultimately happened with the payment Evans (and Holmes?) demanded from the film’s producer for their participation. Was it paid or not? Was it paid and handed over to charity? I’d like to see the receipts.

With its overly sympathetic portrayal of Nerd Jesus Bankman-Fried, could dollars buried somewhere offshore, missed by the Feds, have helped further widen The Altruist’s reach?  This could’ve been done not by the show’s producers or Netflix, but with some third-party publicity help from Bankman-Fried’s team to generate glowing press and viewership for the television series, and its more sympathetic view of him.

You Can See Everything and The Altruist are among the most original and entertaining film and television releases of 2026. Fielder’s nearly three-hour opus flies by like a fever dream, a wonderfully unsettling new form of cinema, a Venus flytrap of a film with a heavy taste of Hitchcock’s Vertigo. With the great shapeshifter Julia Garner in one of the two central roles, The Altruist drags you on a roller coaster, repeatedly swinging from the story’s beginning to its concluding chapters and back again, before the car you’re riding in is catapulted off the tracks. 

In these cases, however, it’s best to look under the hood and separate fact from the entertaining propaganda designed to open the bars for each of its central figures at Club Fed.
