Prince George’s County organizations offer Thanksgiving meals to combat food insecurity

Amid rising food insecurity in Prince George’s County, local humanitarian organizations are working to provide meals for residents during the holidays.

Lines of cars wrapped around the parking lot of University United Methodist Church Sunday, where the College Park Community Food Bank provided 430 families with bags of Thanksgiving-themed foods.

Prince George’s County has long struggled with high rates of food insecurity, with rising levels since the pandemic. This year the number of residents who reported being food insecure hit 56 percent, a five-year high, according to a Capital Area Food Bank study published in September.

This measure is up more than 16 percent from 2022. According to the report, Prince William’s County in Virginia has the second-highest rate of food insecurity in the D.C.-Maryland-Virginia region at 47 percent.

“I am keenly aware that my organization is a band-aid,” College Park Community Food Bank board president Lisa Bartusek said. “The best-case scenario is we put ourselves out of business and don’t have to do it anymore.”

Capital Area Food Bank was one of several partners working with College Park Community Food Bank to make the free drive-up event possible. The food bank also regularly receives contributions through a donation shed, MOM’s Organic Market and other local sources such as farmers.

The food bank is committed to maintaining volunteer-only operations to ensure any money it brings in goes back to purchasing more food, according to Bartusek.

Mission of Love Charities, which is based in Capitol Heights, hosted a similar event on Saturday, providing 200 food baskets to local families at its Thanksgiving Food Basket Giveaway.

About 55 volunteers helped distribute Thanksgiving food staples at the food bank in College Park, including full frozen turkeys, stuffing, cranberry sauce, corn, green beans and gravy, which accompanied the charity’s typical weekly distribution of canned goods and pantry staples.

“The price of food right now, I think, is what’s driving low-income families to our doors more than ever, because it’s harder,” Mission of Love Charities CEO Adenia Bradley said. “Money is not stretching like it used to, and so we do equip the community with the staples.”

In neighboring cities such as Hyattsville, Glenn Dale and Upper Marlboro, other organizations are arranging Thanksgiving food assistance drives to try to tackle short-term effects of county food insecurity during the holidays.

Despite these efforts, experts say larger systemic issues such as health disparities and rising living costs are primary contributors to a lack of accessible food for county residents.

“When you talk about the root causes of food insecurity, one of those solutions that’s often talked about is financial wealth, wealth building, and having economic viability is key,” Evelyn Kelly, the Institute for Public Health Innovation’s strategic initiatives director, said. “For communities who don’t have that, you’re constantly going to be on this roller coaster of never knowing where your next meal may come from.”

The institute is the fiscal sponsor for the Prince George’s County Food Equity Council, which was formed in 2013 to promote equity through changes in the county’s food system, according to the institute’s website.

Since then, the council has engaged in food security initiatives such as providing cold food storage to local food banks and organizations. They are also trying to establish a county-wide food bank, something that similar-sized Maryland counties such as Montgomery County and Anne Arundel County have, according to the council’s senior coordinator Bahati Kalu.

“There are a lot of newer groups who are impacted right now. People who lost federal jobs, adults, immigrant families, of course, and especially children,” Kalu said. “There’s just an increase right now of what’s always been the problem systemically. It’s just getting worse.”

In the region, Black and Hispanic residents are disproportionately impacted by food insecurity, according to the Capital Area Food Bank report.

Increased awareness and input into county policy decisions could create more permanent solutions to the county’s food insecurity problem, according to Kelly. The institute plans to publish a report in January further detailing the state of food insecurity in Prince George’s County and recommending how to move forward, she said.

Residents can support those affected by food insecurity by donating to community distributors and food assistance providers. County residents can find local provider locations through the Prince George’s County Food Equity Council’s website.
