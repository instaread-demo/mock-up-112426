Meet the new Jefferson County Attorney

Louisville Public Media | By Jacob Ryan
Published November 24, 2026 at 4:00 AM EST
  • Facebook
  • Bluesky
  • Twitter
  • LinkedIn
  • Email

There’s a new county attorney in town. Get to know Sarah Martin.

Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 8:40
Instaread

Sarah Martin will become the next Jefferson County Attorney on Jan. 4. She will replace Mike O’Connell, who is retiring after holding the county attorney job since 2008.

Martin beat out Karl Price in the May primary and went into November’s general election uncontested.

She’s not new to the work. Martin has spent 18 years working in the Jefferson County Attorney’s office. She’s been a criminal prosecutor, she’s worked on discrimination cases, she’s advised the Metro Council and she currently is the director of the office’s civil division.

The county attorney’s office plays a major role in the criminal justice system. The office handled nearly 100,000 cases in 2025, according to statistics provided by the agency. The county attorney prosecutes misdemeanor cases in Jefferson District Court, handles family court, juvenile cases, child support and is the legal representative for Metro Government and Metro Council.

Martin sat down for an interview with LPM about her plans for the job, the challenges ahead, and the county attorney’s role in addressing inequity in the criminal justice system.

This transcript is edited for length and clarity.

Jake Ryan: What's the first thing that you want to do?

Sarah Martin: Well, what all my prosecutors will be talking about is the reorganization that's happening in Jefferson District Court, which the county attorney's office is very excited about. Under the leadership of Judge Jessica Moore and Justice Angela Bisig and Clerk David Nicholson, they are implementing a district court reorganization that'll mirror the other 119 district courts across the state. So each judge will manage his or her own docket, and we think that'll improve district court operations significantly. And we're excited to be part of that.

JR: Mike O'Connell has led the office for a long time. How are people going to know there's been a change? Will people know there's been a change?

SM: Well, we are undergoing a review of our criminal court guidelines, so I think there'll be some minor tweaks as we evaluate what needs to be improved. But you know, I've been proud to be part of the leadership team at Mike O'Connell's office for the past seven years, and work here for the past 18 years. And so, I think Mr. O'Connell's done a great job in managing the county attorney's office, and I intend to improve on his operations as we move forward. I don't think there'll be any significant overhauls. We think we run a pretty tight ship around here, and transition is a good time to change.

I served as a prosecutor with Jefferson Commonwealth Attorney Gerina Whethers, and even graduated law school with her. So improving communications and operations between the felony prosecution and misdemeanor prosecution. You know, the county attorney's office handles all the preliminary matters for the felony cases, and so we think there's room for improvement there. And Gerina Whethers and I are excited to improve on the coordination between our offices.

JR: What do you think could be better there?

SM: I think more discussions over what cases they're sending up, and what cases we’re amending down. Communications with the victims of certain crimes. So, yeah, Gerina and I are excited to work more together.

JR: You've led the civil division most recently, and over the past several years, Louisville Metro has paid out many millions of dollars in civil settlements. How do you balance being the city’s lawyer and getting justice for the people that are suing the city?

SM: On the civil side, we have roughly 50 attorneys who handle all of the work of Louisville Metro Government and its elected officials. A number of people sue Louisville Metro Government, and we have roughly about a dozen attorneys who defend the city in those cases. Each of those cases we evaluate individually, and we rely on Mayor Craig Greenberg to decide whether or not to settle a case or whether we go to trial.

JR: Do you think people of color are disproportionately charged with crimes and incarcerated in Louisville?

SM: I think the Louisville Metro Police Department is improving its operations tremendously, and we're proud to be partners with LMPD as they constitutionally police the people in the community. We've got seven attorneys that are embedded in the police department, and we assist LMPD in enforcing the law.

JR: Do you agree with the findings in the 2023 DOJ report on LMPD?

SM: The county attorney's office, we were part of the team that negotiated the settlement, and we were proud of the consent decree that was reached. We entered that in court, and we were very disappointed that President Donald Trump dismissed that case. Mayor Greenberg has continued the promises he made to the community, enforcing the terms that were reached in that consent decree. And I think that that's a good idea. I think there were improvements at LMPD that were important to be made and my office is proud to be part of that reform.

JR: What role do you have in addressing the perception that certain demographics are disproportionately charged and incarcerated?

SM: Well, we're part of the criminal justice system, so if there are citations issued that our prosecutors see that there's a problem with how law enforcement has charged someone, you know, they're going to take that to our leadership to talk about. We're not going to play a role in disproportionately incarcerating or charging people in Jefferson County, and I think that's why Mike O'Connell stopped prosecuting for marijuana under an ounce. That was the reason, because it was disproportionately being issued to African Americans in this community, and he didn't want to have any part of that.

JR: And you’ll continue that policy?

SM: I don't have any intention of changing that policy.

JR: Are you considering deprioritizing the prosecution of crimes that are often tied to poverty or homelessness, like shoplifting or criminal trespassing third?

SM: We will look at — as part of our overall review of the criminal guidelines — whether or not we see any disproportionate charges against those who shouldn't be prosecuted.

JR: What can your office do to ensure that people with mental health issues are receiving fair treatment in the criminal justice system.

SM: So the county attorney's office played a major role in this past General Assembly in changing the law for mental health treatment. We're in the process of training prosecutors. Our assistant county attorney Lauren Mumford even testified in Frankfort to expand the definition and to allow the definitions under the law to allow more people into mental health treatment and to give judges and the county attorney's office more discretion in ensuring that people experiencing mental health crises have the mental health care that they need.

JR: What do you think of the so-called school-to-prison pipeline, and what will you do to ensure Black and Brown kids in Louisville are not disproportionately impacted by the criminal justice system?

SM: Well, one of the ways the county attorney's office touches the school system is with prosecuting parents who don't send their kids to school, and we charge them with truancy. And making sure the parents ensure that children are in school is the best way to lift children out of poverty.

JR: A recent report highlighted the disparities in how LMPD conducts traffic stops, with Black residents facing the brunt of the trend. What's your office's role in addressing that issue?

SM: As part of the consent decree, there were certain guidelines that were agreed to and performance measures that will be evaluated under the independent monitor. I know Chief Paul Humphrey is committed to seeing an improvement in that number, and so is the county attorney's office.

JR: What's your view on prosecuting lower level traffic offenses, like driving without a license, suspended license, expired tags, things like that.

SM: Well, they are a violation of the law, and we intend to continue prosecuting them.

JR: So was possession of marijuana.

SM: But that was disproportionately being charged to African Americans in the community, which is why Mike O'Connell decided to no longer prosecute them.

JR: What about the traffic offenses that might be tied to the disproportionality found in that LMPD stop?

SM: I'm not sure I've seen the breakdown and whether or not those were for not having a license or not wearing a seatbelt or those lower level offenses.

JR: Would you support the elimination of cash bond requirements?

SM: Yes, I think we should go to the system that the federal court system has.
