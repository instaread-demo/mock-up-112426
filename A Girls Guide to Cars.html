This Is How BMWs Become The Ultimate Driving Machine(s) — On a Former Peach Farm!

Over 25,000 peach trees once stood where BMW's newest South Carolina Plant Woodruff—the new plant for building EV batteries in the US

Where 25,000 peach trees once stood, BMW is now cultivating the future of its American EV lineup. On November 13, the automaker celebrated the grand opening of BMW Plant Woodruff, a $700 million, one-million-square-foot facility in Woodruff, South Carolina.

As part of a broader $1.7 billion investment in the region, the fossil-fuel-free plant will be the place of assembly for BMW’s sixth-generation high-voltage batteries—powering upcoming electric X models, starting with the all-new 2027 BMW iX5. From here, those 144-kWh battery packs travel just 15 miles up the road to BMW Plant Spartanburg, where the iX5 becomes BMW’s first EV assembled in the United States.

Joining a group tour of the company’s two plants in Woodruff, S.C. and Spartanburg S.C, I got to see what happens “Beyond the Machine.” Between the 250 robots, AI-monitored assembly lines, and the people bringing it all together, every step pointed toward the secret to the brand’s sauce: excellence and precision in manufacturing leads to excellence on the road.

This story is 100% human-researched and written based on actual first-person knowledge, extensive experience and expertise on the subject of cars and trucks. I was BMW’s guest for this factory tour, but all opinions and impressions are my own.

There’s More Than Just Car Assembly Going on at BMW Plant Woodruff

This was my first visit to a vehicle assembly plant, and it wasn’t what I expected. At 10 million square feet (equivalent to 208 football fields), with more than 11,000 associates, two paint shops and two body shops, the facility seemed endless. The space was bright, and you could hear the machinery, but it wasn’t overwhelming. 

On the assembly floor, associates rode conveyors alongside the cars they were working on. Most of the vehicles had no doors because they traveled separately overhead, giving associates easy access to the interior. At one point, cars were tilted onto their sides so the associates could access components underneath. Nearby, robots lowered panoramic roofs and windows into place. 

Then, at the end of the line, at what’s known as ‘the final finish line’, the cars did something I didn’t expect to see. The BMW iX5’s drove themselves off of the assembly line, which you can see right here.

Your BMW iX5 May Have Driven Itself Before You Did

Toward the end of the assembly process at BMW Plant Spartanburg, the freshly built cars can drive themselves. Traditionally, an associate would have to drive each car from point A to point B, go back to the starting point, and repeat the process. 

Through its collaboration with Embotech, a company that develops autonomous driving technology, BMW now uses automated driving in five plants worldwide—one of them now being BMW Plant Woodruff. Every day, about 3,500 BMWs drive themselves across plant floors around the world.

This technology is designed with safety in mind, and during our tour, we witnessed an unplanned safety demonstration. As a car left the final line, it sensed someone standing nearby. Even though the person was inside the designated safe zone, outside the car’s path, the car stopped and honked its horn anyway. Once the person stepped back, the car continued to its next destination. 

Adopting this technology shows BMW’s commitment to continuous improvement, not because something was broken, but because it could be better.

The Battery Assembly Process is Smooth, Efficient, and Quiet

BMW Plant Woodruff had a similar atmosphere to Spartanburg, but a different kind of energy. It was less of a visual showpiece; there were no driverless cars, no SUVs on conveyors. Instead, the focus was on what powers the 456-mile range-having BMW iX5: the battery. With 300 employees and 250 robots, nearly a 1:1 ratio, the plant was impressive in its own way.

Every step of the battery assembly process was done with accuracy and precision. Materials must meet BMW’s specifications and pass testing before being used. Quality checkpoints are integrated at every step of the assembly process and each battery undergoes roughly 200 tests before leaving the facility, supporting BMW’s goal of zero-defect production.

At the end of the line, finished batteries were stacked in an area that looked like an indoor loading dock, ready for the 15-mile truck ride to BMW Plant Spartanburg, where they will be installed into BMW iX5’s. Currently, the batteries are assembled in Woodruff, but the battery cells are manufactured in China. BMW plans to begin using cells produced in Arizona in the late 2020s.

BMW Was Busy with Plant Woodruff Before it was Built

At Plant Woodruff, excellence began well before the plant was complete. While the building was still under construction, employees were already training through virtual reality. Wearing virtual reality headsets, associates entered a virtual factory that mimicked their future workstations to a tee, so in a sense, they had already worked at their stations before ever stepping into the real plant.

According to BMW, this virtual factory allowed associates to train sooner, faster, and in a low-stress environment.

The Company Says It’s Taking Quality over Quantity Seriously—And I Saw That in Action

BMW also uses technology to help associates maintain quality standards. BMW Plant Spartanburg ships vehicles to over 120 countries and regions, and each car goes through roughly 100 inspection checks covering everything from doors, gas caps, sun visors, and windshield wipers. 

With Plant Spartanburg producing about 1,500 cars a day, the work can be repetitive, so BMW asked, “How can we help the people doing the checks?” The answer is a pilot program called Spatial Human Amplification. Collaborating with academic partners like MIT and Clemson, BMW is testing small clip-on cameras worn by associates on the manufacturing floor during the final quality check.

For example, the camera might capture an associate checking that the windshield wipers function properly. This AI technology would recognize that action as a wiper check and mark the task complete.

The Program is Proving to be Quite Accurate

BMW Plant Spartanburg’s trial of the program achieved 98.33% accuracy in identifying the action being inspected (for example, wipers versus sun visors), following a roughly 99% accuracy rate during a 5 Series test at another plant.

“How can we integrate them [cameras] smoothly into the uniform so that it makes it easy for the associates but still works?” asked Burak Söhmelioglu, Manager of Research & Development for the Americas & Manager of Emerging Technologies. To bridge that gap, BMW is collaborating with the Savannah College of Art & Design (SCAD) to integrate the hardware directly into a “smart uniform.”

Even the uniform is part of getting every detail right. Across both Woodruff and Spartanburg, going “Beyond the Machine” revealed that while the robotics and batteries power the 2027 iX5, it’s the relentless focus on people and precision that defines BMW’s next chapter in South Carolina.
